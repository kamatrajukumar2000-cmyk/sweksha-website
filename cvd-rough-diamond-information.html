CVD Rough Diamond – Growth, Inspection & Quality

Scientific overview of the as-grown CVD diamond crystal, its layered growth, visual inspection, defects, stress and downstream preparation.

← Back to Sweksha website

1. What is CVD rough diamond?

CVD rough diamond is the as-grown diamond crystal produced on a seed substrate before cutting and polishing. Depending on the reactor, recipe, seed and run duration, the crystal may have a plate-like, block-like or other growth morphology. Its appearance alone does not establish its purity, color grade, structural quality or commercial value.

Illustrative CVD rough diamond photograph.

2. Layer-by-layer deposition

In CVD growth, carbon-bearing reactive species generated in the plasma reach the heated diamond surface. Surface reactions add carbon to the existing diamond lattice. Hydrogen participates in the plasma chemistry and helps influence the balance between diamond growth and non-diamond carbon formation. Growth rate, morphology and quality depend on gas composition, pressure, substrate temperature, plasma distribution, surface condition and reactor-specific parameters.

Growth is not perfectly described by one universal recipe. Each reactor and substrate configuration requires a qualified process window and controlled records. Changing gas flow, power, pressure or temperature without a documented reason can make results difficult to interpret and may create safety risks.

3. Post-growth handling and inspection

After the run, the system must be brought to a safe state and vented according to the equipment procedure. The crystal and holder may remain hot; allow the prescribed cooling period before handling. Record the run ID and substrate identity, then inspect the as-grown material for visible cracks, edge damage, surface deposits, non-uniform growth, discoloration and separation from the seed or holder.

Useful inspection records

  • Crystal dimensions, thickness and mass where measured.
  • Photographs of top, side and interface surfaces.
  • Visible cracks, inclusions, color zones or surface irregularities.
  • Run history and any alarms or process deviations.
  • Any measurement or characterization method used and its calibration status.

4. Defects and possible process relationships

Cracks or fracture

May be associated with stress, thermal gradients, handling, substrate condition or geometry.

Non-uniform thickness

May reflect plasma distribution, temperature profile, positioning or process stability.

Dark or non-diamond deposits

May indicate contamination or an imbalance in surface chemistry; characterization is needed.

Inclusions or visible features

Require suitable optical or advanced characterization before assigning a cause.

These are possible relationships, not a diagnosis. Confirm causes using process records and appropriate material characterization.

5. From rough crystal to finished stone

Downstream operations can include removal from the growth assembly, trimming or laser sawing, shaping, polishing and grading. The route depends on crystal geometry, stress, inclusions, color distribution, target dimensions and yield objectives. A cutting plan should be based on measured material characteristics and qualified planning rather than appearance alone.

6. Quality characterization

Depending on the purpose, evaluation may include optical microscopy, dimensional measurement, spectroscopy, photoluminescence, Raman analysis or other laboratory methods. Each method answers different questions. A photograph cannot replace instrument-based characterization or a recognized grading report.

For reliable comparison, preserve traceability from seed lot and reactor run through rough-crystal inspection and downstream processing.
